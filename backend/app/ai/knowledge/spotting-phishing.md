# Spotting Phishing Emails: The Basics

Check the sender. Expand the display name to see the real address. Look-alike domains swap characters (rn for m, 0 for o) or add words such as "secure" or "support".

Check links before clicking. Hover to see the real destination. Read the domain from right to left, starting at the first single slash: login.example.com.evil.example belongs to evil.example.

Watch for pressure. Urgent deadlines, threats of account suspension and requests for secrecy are designed to stop you thinking.

Look at what is being asked. Requests for passwords, MFA codes, payment, gift cards or bank-detail changes are high-risk, whoever appears to be asking.

Other clues include generic greetings such as "Dear customer", unexpected attachments (especially .zip, .html, .iso or macro-enabled Office files), and offers that are too good to be true.

Authentication headers help too. SPF, DKIM and DMARC results of "fail" mean the receiving server could not confirm the sender's domain.

When in doubt, report it. Reporting is always the right call.
